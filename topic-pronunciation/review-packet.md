# THE SLIDES

## Slide 01  (wiki pages: spelling-vs-sound)
# French doesn't sound like it looks
- In English you guess sounds from letters. In French that habit fails.
- French letters don't match sounds one by one.
- Good news: the mismatch follows patterns you can learn.
- Always learn a word together with its sound.
🔊 parle / parles / parlent ≈ parl /paʁl/

## Slide 02  (wiki pages: spelling-vs-sound, ipa)
# Reading French = patterns + IPA
- Learn the spelling patterns in this deck.
- Check new words in IPA: one symbol per sound.
- Symbols you'll meet: /y/ (u), /ʁ/ (r), and nasal vowels with a tilde like /ɔ̃/.
🔊 bon ≈ bohn /bɔ̃/
🔊 rue ≈ rü /ʁy/

## Slide 03  (wiki pages: silent-letters)
# Rule 1: the end of a word is often silent
- Final consonants are usually silent.
- Except c, r, f, l, which are usually pronounced: remember **CaReFuL**.
- The verb ending -ent is always silent.
- ⚠ Sources disagree: -er words like parler break CaReFuL (the r is silent).
🔊 petit ≈ puh-TEE /pəti/
🔊 avec ≈ ah-VEK /avɛk/
🔊 parler ≈ par-LAY /paʁle/

## Slide 04  (wiki pages: schwa, silent-letters)
# Rule 2: the disappearing e
- A final -e is usually silent, unless it's the only vowel (je, que).
- But it makes the consonant before it heard: petit vs petite.
- Unstressed e in the middle of a word often vanishes too. It's called the "mute e" (e muet).
🔊 petite ≈ puh-TEET /pətit/
🔊 je ≈ zhuh /ʒə/
🔊 appeler ≈ ap-LAY /aple/

## Slide 05  (wiki pages: vowel-spellings)
# Vowel letter combos
- Several letters often make one vowel sound.
- Some letters have two possible sounds; the type of syllable decides which.
- Easy anchors: /ɔ/ is like "aw" in saw, /œ/ like the "i" in sir.
🔊 bonjour ≈ bohn-ZHOOR /bɔ̃ʒuʁ/
🔊 moi ≈ mwah /mwa/
🔊 beau ≈ boh /bo/

## Slide 06  (wiki pages: front-rounded-vowels)
# The u sound English doesn't have
- French u is not "oo".
- Say "ee", keep your tongue there, then round your lips like a kiss.
- Mixing up u and ou changes the word.
🔊 tu ≈ tü /ty/
🔊 tout ≈ too /tu/

## Slide 07  (wiki pages: nasal-vowels)
# Nasal vowels: say it through your nose
- Some air goes out through your nose.
- Spelled vowel + n or m, but you **don't** say the n.
- No nasal if a vowel follows: bon (nasal) vs bonne (not nasal).
- ⚠ 3 or 4 nasal vowels? Sources disagree: around Paris most people merge un into in.
🔊 beau ≈ boh /bo/
🔊 bon ≈ bohn /bɔ̃/
🔊 vin ≈ va(n) /vɛ̃/

## Slide 08  (wiki pages: french-r, consonant-spellings)
# The French r, h and j
- **r**: made at the back of the mouth. Start from an English "h" and tighten your throat.
- Don't stress: any r is understandable.
- **h** is always silent.
- **j** sounds like the s in "pleasure".
🔊 rue ≈ rü /ʁy/
🔊 hôtel ≈ oh-TEL /otɛl/
🔊 je ≈ zhuh /ʒə/

## Slide 09  (wiki pages: accents)
# What accents do
- **é** = "ay": été.
- **ç** (cedilla) makes c sound like s.
- **à / où**: the grave accent just tells words apart.
- **ê, ô** (circumflex): often marks a lost s (fête ← feste); doesn't always change the sound.
🔊 été ≈ ay-TAY /ete/
🔊 garçon ≈ gar-SOHN /ɡaʁsɔ̃/

## Slide 10  (wiki pages: liaison)
# Liaison: linking words together
- A silent final consonant wakes up before a vowel. s and x sound like z.
- **Always** link: determiner + noun, pronoun + verb.
- **Never** link after et ("and").
- ⚠ Optional liaisons sound formal; beginners can skip them.
🔊 les amis ≈ lay-za-MEE /lezami/
🔊 vous avez ≈ voo-za-VAY /vuzave/

## Slide 11  (wiki pages: stress)
# Rhythm: no word stress
- English stresses one syllable per word (PHO-to-graph). French doesn't.
- Every syllable keeps its full vowel.
- A light stress falls on the last syllable of a word group.
🔊 photographie ≈ fo-to-gra-FEE /fɔtɔɡʁafi/

## Slide 12  (wiki pages: silent-letters, liaison)
# Read one sentence, step by step
**Ils parlent avec les amis.** (They talk with the friends.)
1. ils: final s silent before a consonant.
2. parlent: -ent is silent.
3. avec: c is pronounced (CaReFuL).
4. les amis: liaison, s becomes z.
🔊 Ils parlent avec les amis ≈ eel parl ah-vek lay-za-mee /il paʁl avɛk lezami/

## Slide 13  (wiki pages: nasal-vowels, liaison, front-rounded-vowels, silent-letters)
# Top 4 beginner mistakes
1. Adding an "n" after a nasal vowel: bon is not "bonn".
2. Saying u like "oo": tu ≠ tout.
3. Pronouncing every final letter: parlent ends in "l".
4. Linking after et: Paul et Anne has no t.
🔊 Paul et Anne ≈ pol ay ahn /pɔl e an/

## Slide 14  (wiki pages: spelling-vs-sound, silent-letters, nasal-vowels, liaison)
# Summary
- French spelling is patterned, not random.
- The end of a word is often silent (CaReFuL, -ent, -e).
- Nasal vowels: air through the nose, no n.
- Liaison: silent consonants wake up before vowels.
- Learn every word with its sound.

## Slide 15  (wiki pages: silent-letters, nasal-vowels, liaison, front-rounded-vowels)
# Quick quiz
1. How do you say parlent?
2. Which has a nasal vowel: bon or bonne?
3. In les amis, what sound links the words?
4. How is tu different from tout?
Answers: 1. "parl", -ent is silent · 2. bon · 3. a z sound · 4. tu is "ee" with rounded lips

# Spelling vs sound

**One line:** French letters don't map one-to-one to sounds, but the patterns are learnable.

## Explanation
English speakers try to read French with English letter rules, which fails. French spelling
keeps many letters that are no longer pronounced, so you need to learn spelling *patterns*
and always pair a word with a spoken model.

## Key points
- Letters and sounds don't line up one by one. [S1 §Prononciation — Les bases] «French spelling and pronunciation do not correspond letter by letter»
- Learn with audio: read aloud, record yourself, repeat. [S1 §Prononciation — Les bases] «Use these patterns with a spoken model»
- Unlike English, French is predictable from spelling. [S2 §dear duolingo] «French pronunciation is actually predictable from spelling»

## Examples
- `parle / parles / parlent` all sound the same ≈ parl /paʁl/

# IPA (International Phonetic Alphabet)

**One line:** one symbol per sound, so you can write pronunciation exactly, e.g. bon = /bɔ̃/.

## Explanation
Because French spelling hides the sound, dictionaries write words in IPA between slashes.
Each symbol is always the same sound. Beginners don't need all of them; the useful ones are
the sounds English lacks: /y/ (u), /ʁ/ (r), and the nasal vowels with a tilde, like /ɔ̃/.

## Key points
- A symbol key for French sounds. [S4 §Help:IPA/French] «It provides a set of symbols to represent the pronunciation of French in Wikipedia»
- The r is written /ʁ/. [S4 §Notes] «The French rhotic /ʁ/ is usually guttural (uvular)»

## Examples
- 🔊 `bon` ≈ bohn /bɔ̃/ · 🔊 `rue` ≈ rü /ʁy/

# Silent letters

**One line:** many letters at the end of French words are written but not said.

## Explanation
Final consonants are usually silent, final -e is usually silent, and the verb ending -ent is
silent. Some final consonants *are* pronounced, so beginners need rules of thumb plus memory.
A silent final consonant can "wake up" before a vowel (see liaison).

## Key points
- Final consonants are often silent. [S1 §Silent letters and word endings] «A final written consonant is often silent»
- But some are pronounced. [S1 §Silent letters and word endings] «But some are pronounced: avec /avɛk/, sac /sak/»
- The verb ending -ent is silent. [S1 §Silent letters and word endings] «The verb ending -ent is silent in ils parlent»
- Final -e can make the consonant before it heard. [S1 §Silent letters and word endings] «it can make the preceding consonant audible: petit/petite»
- Final c, r, f, l are usually pronounced (CaReFuL). [S2 §What letters should or shouldn't be pronounced?] «If a word ends with consonants c, r, f, l»
- Other final consonants are not. [S2 §What letters should or shouldn't be pronounced?] «If a word ends with any other consonant»
- Final -e is silent unless it's the only vowel. [S2 §What letters should or shouldn't be pronounced?] «Except when the -e is the only vowel sound in the word»
- Many silent letters come from Latin-based respellings. [S5 §French orthography] «further increased the number of silent letters»

## Examples
- `petit` ≈ puh-TEE /pəti/ vs `petite` ≈ puh-TEET /pətit/

## Related
[[spelling-vs-sound]] · [[liaison]] · [[schwa]]

## Disagreements
- **CaReFuL vs -er words.** S2 says final c, r, f, l are pronounced [S2 §What letters should or shouldn't be pronounced?],
  but its own table shows -er endings like *parler* said as /e/ with no r [S2 §Combinations with "e" at the end of a word].
  Treat CaReFuL as a rule of thumb with exceptions; S1 says to learn each word's sound [S1 §Silent letters and word endings].

# Schwa (e muet, the "mute e")

**One line:** the weak "uh" vowel spelled e, which often disappears in speech.

## Explanation
Many unaccented e's in French are a short, relaxed "uh" sound called schwa. Very often it isn't
pronounced at all, especially in the middle of words and at the end of words. So a word can
have fewer spoken syllables than written ones.

## Key points
- Its other names are e caduc and e muet. [S3 §Schwa] «also called e caduc ('dropped e') and e muet ('mute e')»
- It's "unstable": often dropped. [S3 §Schwa] «The main characteristic of French schwa is its "instability"»
- Often dropped after one consonant mid-word. [S3 §Schwa] «That is usually the case when it follows a single consonant in a medial syllable»

## Examples
- 🔊 `appeler` ≈ ap-LAY (the middle e drops) /aple/

[[silent-letters]] · [[stress]]

# Vowel spellings

**One line:** French writes many vowel sounds with letter pairs (ai, eau, ou, oi).

## Explanation
Several French vowel sounds are spelled with two or three letters. Once you know the common
combinations, you can read most words aloud correctly.

## Key points
- Some vowel letters have more than one sound. [S2 §Vowel spellings and pronunciation] «have multiple pronunciations, how do you know which one to use?»

- /ɔ/ sounds like "aw" in saw. [S2 §Vowel spellings and pronunciation] «makes the same sound as “aw” in American English saw»
- /œ/ sounds like the "i" in sir. [S2 §Vowel spellings and pronunciation] «sounds like “i” in English sir»

# The u sound (front rounded vowels)

**One line:** French u is "ee" said with rounded lips, different from "oo" (ou).

## Explanation
English has no vowel like French u /y/. You make it by putting your tongue where it goes for
"ee" and rounding your lips as for "oo". Mixing up u and ou changes the word.

## Key points
- Tell tu and tout apart. [S1 §Open and closed vowels] «Also distinguish tu /ty/ and tout /tu/»
- Start from /i/ and round your lips. [S1 §Open and closed vowels] «begin with the tongue position of /i/ and round your lips»
- Say "ee" with a kissy face. [S2 §Vowel spellings and pronunciation] «say “ee” like in English see while rounding your lips»

## Examples
- `tu` ≈ tü (ee + round lips) /ty/ vs `tout` ≈ too /tu/

# Nasal vowels

**One line:** vowels where some air goes out through your nose, with no "n" at the end.

## Explanation
French has vowels made with the soft palate lowered, so air flows through the nose too.
They're usually spelled vowel + n or m (an, on, in, un), but the n/m itself isn't pronounced.
The classic beginner mistake is adding an English "n" sound.

## Key points
- Some air flows through the nose. [S1 §Oral and nasal vowels] «For a nasal vowel, some air also flows through the nose»
- Don't add an English n. [S1 §Oral and nasal vowels] «Do not add a separate English n after a nasal vowel»
- Many speakers merge two of them. [S1 §Oral and nasal vowels] «Many speakers merge the last two sounds»
- S2 counts four nasal vowels. [S2 §Vowel + "m" or "n"] «French has 4 “nasal” vowel sounds»
- n/m isn't nasal if a vowel follows. [S2 §Vowel + "m" or "n"] «Not every vowel followed by “m” or “n” becomes nasal»
- Linguists count up to 4 nasal vowels. [S3 §Vowels] «Standard French contrasts up to 13 oral vowels and up to 4 nasal vowels»
- Around Paris most speakers merge un into in. [S3 §Nasal vowels] «many speakers use only the latter phoneme, especially most speakers in northern France such as Paris»
- Outside Paris the pair stays distinct. [S4 §Notes] «These pairs are always distinguished in Northern, Belgian, Swiss, and Quebec French»

## Examples
- `beau` ≈ boh /bo/ vs `bon` ≈ bohn (no n!) /bɔ̃/

## Related
[[spelling-vs-sound]]

## Disagreements
- **Three or four nasal vowels?** S2 says French has four [S2 §Vowel + "m" or "n"]; S1 says many speakers merge
  two of them (un and in) [S1 §Oral and nasal vowels]. S3 explains both are right: the standard has *up to* four [S3 §Vowels], and most speakers
  around Paris merge two [S3 §Nasal vowels]. Beginners can learn three and recognize the fourth.

# The French r

**One line:** a soft, throaty r made at the back of the mouth, not with the tongue tip.

## Explanation
The usual French r /ʁ/ is made where the back of the tongue meets the soft palate, close to
where you gargle. You don't curl the tongue like an English r. Being understood matters more
than a perfect accent.

## Key points
- Made at the back of the mouth. [S1 §The French r] «is made toward the back of the mouth»
- Clarity beats copying an accent. [S1 §The French r] «Clarity matters more than imitating a particular regional accent»
- Start from an English h and tighten the throat. [S2 §Hey, what about the French "r"?] «tightening your throat a little more on the “h.”»
- Any r is understandable. [S2 §Hey, what about the French "r"?] «any “r” will do»
- It's usually uvular, and varies by region. [S4 §Notes] «The French rhotic /ʁ/ is usually guttural (uvular), but it varies by region»

## Examples
- `rue` ≈ rü (throaty r) /ʁy/

# Accents and marks

**One line:** marks on letters that often change the sound (é, è, ç) and sometimes don't (ê, ô).

## Explanation
French uses accents and other marks on letters. Some tell you how to pronounce the vowel, the
cedilla changes c into an s sound, and some marks only distinguish words in writing.

## Key points
- é usually means /e/. [S1 §Accents and other marks] «commonly marks /e/: été»
- A cedilla makes c sound like s. [S1 §Accents and other marks] «A cedilla makes c sound /s/»
- The circumflex doesn't always change the sound. [S1 §Accents and other marks] «it does not always change the sound»
- é represents /e/. [S5 §Diacritics] «An acute accent over ⟨e⟩ represents /e/»
- à vs a and où vs ou only tell words apart. [S5 §Diacritics] «is primarily used to distinguish homophones»
- A circumflex often marks a lost s, as in fête from feste. [S5 §Diacritics] «it indicates the historical deletion of an adjacent letter»
- The cedilla turns c into /s/. [S5 §Diacritics] «A cedilla under ⟨c⟩ indicates that it is pronounced /s/ rather than /k/»

## Examples
- `été` ≈ ay-TAY /ete/ · `garçon` ≈ gar-SOHN /ɡaʁsɔ̃/

# Liaison

**One line:** a normally silent final consonant is pronounced when the next word starts with a vowel.

## Explanation
French links words together. If a word ends in a silent consonant and the next word starts
with a vowel sound, that consonant is often pronounced and glued onto the next word. The s/x
usually comes out as a z sound. Some liaisons are required, some optional, some forbidden.

## Key points
- A silent final consonant becomes audible before a vowel. [S1 §Liaison and intonation] «a normally silent final consonant becomes audible»
- s and x usually sound like z. [S1 §2. Three liaison sounds to practice] «s and x usually sound like [z]»
- Never link after et. [S1 §6. Do not connect every final consonant] «Never make a liaison after et»
- Some H words block liaison. [S1 §4. What about words beginning with H?] «Other H words block a liaison»
- Linguists call it a "latent" final consonant. [S3 §Liaison] «having a "latent" final consonant that is pronounced only in certain syntactic contexts»
- Mandatory: determiner + noun. [S6 §Mandatory liaison] «between a determiner and a following adjective or noun»
- Mandatory: pronoun + verb. [S6 §Mandatory liaison] «between a subject or object pronoun and the verb»
- Optional ones sound formal. [S6 §Optional liaison] «The realization of optional liaisons is a signal of formal register»
- Impossible after et. [S6 §Impossible liaison] «after certain words, for example et ("and"), and all singular nouns»

## Examples
- `les amis` ≈ lay-za-MEE /lezami/

[[silent-letters]] · [[enchainement]]

## Disagreements
- **Should a beginner make optional liaisons?** S6 says pedagogical grammars often recommend making as many

# Stress and rhythm

**One line:** French gives syllables roughly even weight, with a light stress at the end of a word group.

## Explanation
English stresses one syllable per word (PHOtograph vs phoTOgraphy). French doesn't work like
that: stress never changes meaning, vowels keep their full quality in every syllable, and the
emphasis tends to fall on the last full syllable of a group of words.

## Key points
- Stress never distinguishes words. [S3 §Stress] «it has always been agreed that word stress is not distinctive in French»
- Vowels keep full quality everywhere. [S3 §Stress] «vowels keep their full quality in all syllables»
- Stress falls at the end of a word group. [S3 §Stress] «French has stress that falls on the final full syllable of what has been»

## Examples
- 🔊 `photographie` ≈ fo-to-gra-FEE /fɔtɔɡʁafi/

## Related
[[intonation]] · [[schwa]]

## Disagreements
- **Linguists disagree on what French stress is.** S3 reports views from final-syllable word stress
  to no word stress at all [S3 §Stress]. All agree it never changes meaning, which is what a beginner needs.
