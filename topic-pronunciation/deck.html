French doesn't sound like it looks

  • In English you guess sounds from letters. In French that habit fails.
  • French letters don't match sounds one by one.
  • Good news: the mismatch follows patterns you can learn.
  • Always learn a word together with its sound.
parle / parles / parlent≈ parl/paʁl/
Teacher notes: Ask the audience how many sounds they hear in the three verbs. Answer: they're identical.

Rule 1: the end of a word is often silent

  • Final consonants are usually silent.
  • Except c, r, f, l, which are usually pronounced: remember CaReFuL.
  • The verb ending -ent is always silent.
  • Heads up Sources disagree: -er words like parler break CaReFuL (the r is silent).
petit≈ puh-TEE/pəti/
avec≈ ah-VEK/avɛk/
parler≈ par-LAY/paʁle/
Teacher notes: CaReFuL is a rule of thumb, not a law. Learn each word's sound.
Sources: S1 §Silent letters and word endings · S2 §What letters should or shouldn't be pronounced?

04 / 15

Rule 2: the disappearing e

  • A final -e is usually silent, unless it's the only vowel (je, que).
  • But it makes the consonant before it heard: petit vs petite.
  • Unstressed e in the middle of a word often vanishes too. It's called the "mute e" (e muet).
petite≈ puh-TEET/pətit/
je≈ zhuh/ʒə/
appeler≈ ap-LAY/aple/
Teacher notes: Count syllables: appeler is written with 3 vowels but often said with 2.
Sources: S2 §What letters should or shouldn't be pronounced? · S1 §Silent letters and word endings · S3 §Schwa

05 / 15

Vowel letter combos

  • Several letters often make one vowel sound.
  • Some letters have two possible sounds; the type of syllable decides which.
  • Easy anchors: /ɔ/ is like "aw" in saw, /œ/ like the "i" in sir.
bonjour≈ bohn-ZHOOR/bɔ̃ʒuʁ/
moi≈ mwah/mwa/
beau≈ boh/bo/
Teacher notes: ou = "oo", oi = "wa", eau = "oh". Have them read the three examples first.
Sources: S2 §Vowel spellings and pronunciation

06 / 15

The u sound English doesn't have

  • French u is not "oo".
  • Say "ee", keep your tongue there, then round your lips like a kiss.
  • Mixing up u and ou changes the word.
tu≈ tü/ty/
tout≈ too/tu/
Teacher notes: Everyone says "ee", then rounds the lips without moving the tongue.
Sources: S1 §Open and closed vowels · S2 §Vowel spellings and pronunciation

07 / 15

Nasal vowels: say it through your nose

  • Some air goes out through your nose.
  • Spelled vowel + n or m, but you don't say the n.
  • No nasal if a vowel follows: bon (nasal) vs bonne (not nasal).
  • Heads up 3 or 4? Standard French has up to 4, but around Paris most people merge un into in, so you often hear 3.
beau≈ boh/bo/
bon≈ bohn/bɔ̃/
vin≈ va(n)/vɛ̃/
Teacher notes: Pinch your nose on "bon": you should feel it buzz.
Sources: S1 §Oral and nasal vowels · S2 §Vowel + "m" or "n" · S3 §Nasal vowels · S3 §Vowels

08 / 15

The French r, h and j

  • r: made at the back of the mouth. Start from an English "h" and tighten your throat.
  • Don't stress: any r is understandable.
  • h is always silent.
  • j sounds like the s in "pleasure".
rue≈ rü/ʁy/
hôtel≈ oh-TEL/otɛl/
je≈ zhuh/ʒə/
Teacher notes: Practice "hose" → "rose" by tightening the throat.
Sources: S1 §The French r · S2 §Hey, what about the French "r"? · S2 §What sounds do French consonants make?

09 / 15

What accents do

  • é = "ay": été.
  • ç (cedilla) makes c sound like s.
  • à / où: the grave accent just tells words apart.
  • ê, ô (circumflex): often marks a lost s (fête ← feste); doesn't always change the sound.
été≈ ay-TAY/ete/
garçon≈ gar-SOHN/ɡaʁsɔ̃/
Teacher notes: Keep accents when writing; they carry information.
Sources: S5 §Diacritics · S1 §Accents and other marks

10 / 15

Liaison: linking words together

  • In certain spots, a silent final consonant wakes up before a vowel. s and x sound like z.
  • Always link: determiner + noun, pronoun + verb.
  • Never link after et ("and"). Some H words block it too.
  • Heads up Optional liaisons sound formal; beginners can skip them.
les amis≈ lay-za-MEE/lezami/
vous avez≈ voo-za-VAY/vuzave/
Teacher notes: Contrast les amis (link) with les copains (no vowel, no link).
Sources: S1 §Liaison and intonation · S1 §2. Three liaison sounds to practice · S6 §Mandatory liaison · S6 §Impossible liaison · S6 §Optional liaison · S1 §4. What about words beginning with H? · S3 §Liaison

11 / 15

Rhythm: no word stress

  • English stresses one syllable per word (PHO-to-graph). French doesn't.
  • Every syllable keeps its full vowel.
  • A light stress falls on the last syllable of a word group.
photographie≈ fo-to-gra-FEE/fɔtɔɡʁafi/
Teacher notes: Clap the syllables evenly, then add a slight lift at the end.
Sources: S3 §Stress

12 / 15

Read one sentence, step by step

Ils parlent avec les amis. (They talk with the friends.)

  1. ils: final s silent before a consonant.
  2. parlent: -ent is silent.
  3. avec: c is pronounced (CaReFuL).
  4. les amis: liaison, s becomes z.
Ils parlent avec les amis≈ eel parl ah-vek lay-za-mee/il paʁl avɛk lezami/
Teacher notes: Read it slowly, then at normal speed with the liaison glued on.
Sources: S1 §Silent letters and word endings · S2 §What letters should or shouldn't be pronounced? · S1 §Liaison and intonation

13 / 15

Top 4 beginner mistakes

  1. Adding an "n" after a nasal vowel: bon is not "bonn".
  2. Saying u like "oo": tu ≠ tout.
  3. Pronouncing every final letter: parlent is said "parl", the -ent is silent.
  4. Linking after et: Paul et Anne has no t.
Paul et Anne≈ pol ay ahn/pɔl e an/
Teacher notes: Ask which mistake they made today.
Sources: S1 §Oral and nasal vowels · S1 §Open and closed vowels · S1 §Silent letters and word endings · S1 §6. Do not connect every final consonant

Summary

  • French spelling is patterned, not random.
  • Word endings are often silent (-ent, -e), but c, r, f, l are usually heard (CaReFuL).
  • Nasal vowels: air through the nose, no n.
  • Liaison: in certain spots, a silent consonant wakes up before a vowel.
  • Learn every word with its sound.
