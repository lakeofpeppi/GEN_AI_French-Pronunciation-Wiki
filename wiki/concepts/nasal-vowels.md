# Nasal vowels

**One line:** vowels where some air goes out through your nose, with no "n" at the end.

## Explanation
French has vowels made with the soft palate lowered, so air flows through the nose too.
They're usually spelled vowel + n or m (an, on, in, un), but the n/m itself isn't pronounced.
The classic beginner mistake is adding an English "n" sound.

## Key points
- Some air flows through the nose. [S1 §Oral and nasal vowels] «For a nasal vowel, some air also flows through the nose»
- Don't add an English n. [S1 §Oral and nasal vowels] «Do not add a separate English n after a nasal vowel»
- Many speakers merge two of them. [S1 §Oral and nasal vowels] «Many speakers merge the last two sounds»
- S2 counts four nasal vowels. [S2 §Vowel + "m" or "n"] «French has 4 “nasal” vowel sounds»
- n/m isn't nasal if a vowel follows. [S2 §Vowel + "m" or "n"] «Not every vowel followed by “m” or “n” becomes nasal»
- Linguists count up to 4 nasal vowels. [S3 §Vowels] «Standard French contrasts up to 13 oral vowels and up to 4 nasal vowels»
- Around Paris most speakers merge un into in. [S3 §Nasal vowels] «many speakers use only the latter phoneme, especially most speakers in northern France such as Paris»
- Outside Paris the pair stays distinct. [S4 §Notes] «These pairs are always distinguished in Northern, Belgian, Swiss, and Quebec French»

## Examples
- `beau` ≈ boh /bo/ vs `bon` ≈ bohn (no n!) /bɔ̃/

## Disagreements
- **Three or four nasal vowels?** S2 says French has four [S2 §Vowel + "m" or "n"]; S1 says many speakers merge
  two of them (un and in) [S1 §Oral and nasal vowels]. S3 explains both are right: the standard has *up to* four [S3 §Vowels], and most speakers
  around Paris merge two [S3 §Nasal vowels]. Beginners can learn three and recognize the fourth.
